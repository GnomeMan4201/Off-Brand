    How to spot a phish and stay safe

    Phishing pages copy the look of real sites. The look can be copied perfectly, so judge the address and the request instead.

    Check the address

    • Read the domain from right to left. The real owner is the part just before the ending (.com, .pl, .app). In allegro.pl.login-check.top, the owner is login-check.top, not Allegro.
    • A brand name at the start of the address, or in a subdomain, proves nothing. Anyone can put any word there.
    • Be wary of brands on free hosting addresses such as .vercel.app, .netlify.app, .pages.dev, .github.io or .web.app. Large companies use their own domains for sign-in.
    • Look for swapped or extra characters: rn for m, 0 for o, 1 or I for l, extra hyphens, misspellings.
    • The padlock (HTTPS) only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites have padlocks too.
    • On a phone, tap the address bar to see the full address. Phones often cut off the part that matters.

    Check the request

    • Pressure is a warning sign: account locked, payment failed, package held, small fee due, act within 24 hours.
    • No real company will ask for your password, a one-time code or a crypto wallet recovery phrase by message, by phone or on a page you reached from a link.
    • Anyone who asks for a wallet's recovery phrase (seed phrase) is trying to steal the wallet. Hardware wallet makers never need it.
    • Unexpected requests to pay with gift cards, crypto or wire transfers are almost always fraud.

    Habits that stop most phishing

    • Don't sign in from a link in an email, text or ad. Type the address yourself, or use a bookmark or the official app.
    • Use a password manager. It only fills in passwords on the real site, so if it doesn't offer your login, stop and check the address.
    • Turn on passkeys or a hardware security key where offered. They can't be used on a fake site. App-based codes are the next best option; text-message codes can be phished.
    • Use a different password for every account, so one stolen password doesn't open the others.
    • Keep your browser and phone updated. Built-in phishing warnings depend on current lists.

    If you already entered details

    • Go to the real site directly and change the password right away. Change it anywhere else you used the same password.
    • Sign out of other sessions and check recovery email, phone number, forwarding rules and connected apps for changes you didn't make.
    • If you entered card or bank details, call the number on the back of your card or on the bank's official website.
    • If you entered a crypto recovery phrase, assume the wallet is compromised. Move the funds to a new wallet with a new phrase immediately.
    • Turn on two-factor authentication for the account if it isn't on already.

    Report it

    • Phishing emails: forward them to reportphishing@apwg.org (Anti-Phishing Working Group).
    • Phishing websites: report them to Google Safe Browsing, which feeds browser warnings.
    • Scam texts in the US: forward them to 7726 (SPAM).
    • Fraud in the US: reportfraud.ftc.gov. Elsewhere, contact your national fraud or cybercrime reporting centre.
    • Report it to the company being impersonated. Most have a phishing or abuse address on their official site.
